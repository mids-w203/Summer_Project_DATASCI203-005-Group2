Project Raincheck

The loose promise of El Niño, quantified.

Seventy-six California water years, one Pacific thermostat, and the question every reservoir operator asks in October: does the ocean know something we don't?

By Daniel Bowd, Gunhui (Simon) Kim, Rishi Raj & Sasha Schaps
SCROLL ↓

California does not do average. Statewide precipitation over a water year — October through the following September — runs about 22 inches, but the state routinely lands six inches above or below it. Whole reservoirs, hydropower forecasts, and allocation fights live inside that swing, which is why the Department of Water Resources, the Bureau of Reclamation, and PG&E all watch the tropical Pacific every fall.

What they're watching is ENSO — the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, a slow slosh of warm and cool water along the equator. When the Niño 3.4 region runs hot, it's an El Niño; cold, a La Niña; in between, Neutral. Decades of research — Schonher & Nicholson in 1989, Redmond & Koch in 1991, Cayan and colleagues in 1999 — links El Niño winters to wetter Californias.

But the folklore version — El Niño means rain — keeps failing in public. So we asked a more precise question: not which label a year wears, but how much intensity matters. Scroll on.

Before we average the state into a single figure, it's worth remembering what that figure is averaging. Each of these places lives on a different kind of water — and each has a rain gauge with a real record behind it.

INDEX 01 · WHAT THE WATER FEELS LIKEONI — Oceanic Niño Index

A 3-month running mean of sea-surface-temperature anomalies (ERSSTv5) averaged over the Niño 3.4 box of the equatorial Pacific (5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W).

Anomalies are measured against centered 30-year climatologies that NOAA updates every five years — so a warming ocean isn't misread as a permanent El Niño. Our predictor, oni_djf, is the December–February value.

INDEX 02 · WHAT THE AIR IS DOINGrSOI — reversed Southern Oscillation Index

The SOI is pressure-based: standardized sea-level pressure at Tahiti minus Darwin, as departures from the 1981–2010 base period (NOAA NCEI). Negative SOI coincides with El Niño — the opposite sign convention to the ONI.

So rsoi is the November–March mean SOI × (−1): positive means El Niño for both indices in this report.

Six models, one table

One row per water year, WY 1950–2025. Outcome: statewide precipitation (NOAA Climate at a Glance). Predictor: December–February Oceanic Niño Index (NOAA CPC), kept continuous — a weak and a very strong El Niño are different events — with the pressure-based rSOI joining in the final column as a cross-check. Economists call what follows a stargazer table: every model in its own column, every coefficient wearing its standard error underneath and its stars beside. It rewards slow reading — so read it slowly. Scroll, and the table will follow along.

How to hold it

Rows are variables; columns are models. Each cell shows the coefficient, its HC1 robust standard error in parentheses, and stars for significance (* p<0.1, ** p<0.05, *** p<0.01).

Robust errors are the seatbelt: they never change where the estimate lands, only how confidently we're allowed to talk about it — and the fan in the scatter above is why we buckle up.

Model 1 · the headline

+2.550 in/°C

Intensity, kept continuous. Each additional °C of winter ONI associates with about two and a half more inches of statewide rain, and the estimate clears every conventional threshold (robust p < 0.001).

This is the estimand. Everything else in the table is a stress test of this number.

Model 2 · the folklore, formalized

Replace the thermometer with the labels — El Niño and La Niña dummies against a Neutral baseline — and the stars mostly go out: El Niño p = 0.079, La Niña p = 0.399, R² drops to 0.108.

Binning at ±0.5 °C files 2016's monstrous +2.6 °C in the same drawer as a barely-there +0.5 °C. Intensity is the signal; the phase name is mostly noise.

Model 3 · the stress test

Could last winter's ocean, or a slow secular trend, be confounding the slope? Add both. Neither earns a star (lag p = 0.30, trend p = 0.54) — and the ONI slope barely moves: 2.550 → 2.750.

That stability across specifications is the robustness argument. Parsimony sends us back to Model 1.

Model 4 · the same tilt, in percent

Log the outcome and the coefficient changes language: 100·(e0.105 − 1) ≈ 11.1% more rain per °C, multiplicatively.

Two catches travel with the transform: back-transformed predictions are geometric means (slightly below the arithmetic means planners budget with), and its R² lives on a different outcome scale — it cannot be compared to the other columns.

Model 5 · the bend test

Do the monster winters pull harder than a straight line allows? Add ONI². The curvature points the expected way — extremes wetter than the line predicts — but it never earns a star (robust p ≈ 0.19), and the linear term barely moves.

A line survives its stress test. A true threshold response, if one exists, would need more than 76 winters to convict.

Model 6 · two thermometers, one ocean

Nest the pressure-based rSOI — and the El Niño label — beside the ONI. Every star goes out, the ONI's included, because ONI and rSOI move together at r = 0.87: near-collinearity splits one signal across two coefficients and inflates every standard error.

The lesson isn't "no effect." It's that the second index carries no information the first didn't already have — R² barely moves (0.199 vs 0.187) while the noise doubles.

The bottom rows do the judging

Model 3 wins raw R² (0.218), but raw R² can only rise when you add regressors. Adjusted R² charges rent for each extra term — and after rent, the quadratic's 0.188 and Model 3's 0.184 sit barely above Model 1's 0.176, so the extra coefficients barely pay for themselves.

And every column agrees on the ceiling: ENSO explains roughly a fifth of the variance. The other four-fifths is everything ENSO is not.

Diagnostics, not vibes

With n = 76 we lean on the large-sample OLS assumptions — which means normal errors are a courtesy, not a requirement, and the real work is checking the other four honestly.

1 · IID sampling

Partly

Water years form a time series, not a random draw. The dependence looks mild — residual lag-1 autocorrelation ≈ 0.08, Durbin–Watson = 1.82 — but robust SEs fix heteroskedasticity, not serial correlation.

2 · No perfect collinearity

Met

ONI, its lag, and the time trend are nearly orthogonal (all VIFs ≈ 1); every design matrix is full rank.

3 · Linear conditional expectation

Met (approx.)

A quadratic ONI term is insignificant (robust p ≈ 0.19), so a line is a fair approximation — though a threshold response can't be ruled out at this sample size.

4 · Homoskedasticity

Violated → handled

Breusch–Pagan p = 0.033 and the visible fan at high ONI reject constant variance. This is why every standard error on this page is HC1 robust.

5 · Normal errors

Not needed (n = 76)

Model 1 residuals pass Shapiro–Wilk comfortably (p = 0.97); Model 4's less so (p = 0.015). Either way the CLT carries the inference.

What each model cannot tell you

Limitations here are design trade-offs, not mistakes — every specification buys its insight by giving something up.

1 · Linear

  • R² = 0.19: four-fifths of any year is atmospheric rivers and storm-track luck — everything ENSO is not.
  • 1983 carries real leverage (Cook's D ≈ 0.29 at n = 76); the slope leans on two monster winters.
  • Forces one constant inches-per-°C effect across the whole ONI range; a threshold response would be invisible.

2 · Phase dummies

  • Discards intensity: +2.6 °C (2016) and +0.5 °C land in the same bucket.
  • ±0.5 °C cutoffs are a NOAA convention, not an estimate — results inherit that arbitrariness.
  • Weakest fit (R² = 0.108) and no coefficient clears p < 0.05: labels alone ≈ noise.

3 · Lag + trend

  • Neither added term is significant (lag p = 0.30, trend p = 0.54): extra complexity, no extra insight.
  • Drops WY1950 (n = 75) and burns two more degrees of freedom in a small sample.
  • Adjusted-R² edge over Model 1 is marginal (0.184 vs 0.176); parsimony favors Model 1.

4 · Log-linear

  • Back-transformed predictions are geometric means — systematically below the arithmetic mean planners budget with.
  • Residual normality is worse in logs (Shapiro p = 0.015); the transform wasn't needed for skew here.
  • Its R² is not comparable to the level-outcome models: the outcome variable itself changed.

5 · Quadratic

  • The curvature never earns a star (robust p ≈ 0.19): a bend may be real, but this sample can't prove it.
  • The squared term leans on the same handful of very strong events that give 1983 its leverage in the line.
  • Even if the bend were confirmed, a planner still can't act on it without knowing the winter's ONI in advance.

6 · ONI + rSOI + phase

  • ONI and rSOI correlate at 0.87 — the design is nearly collinear, and every standard error inflates.
  • The El Niño dummy is redundant beside two continuous indices; its coefficient even flips sign.
  • Best read as a robustness check, not a candidate model: it shows the result isn't an artifact of one index.

Shared limitation: statewide averaging dilutes a signal Jong et al. (2016) show is concentrated in Southern California — and none of this is causal or predictive.

If the promise is loose, what do you do with it?

Two places where the answer matters in practice: managing water, and building the infrastructure that depends on it. In both, the models above argue for the same stance — treat an ENSO forecast as a tilt in the odds, and let the extremes, not the phase-conditioned averages, set the hard constraints.

Water supply: plan on distributions

  • A seasonal ENSO outlook arrives in fall, before the water year — real lead time. But with R² ≈ 0.19, it should move readiness (staffing, wet-year capture permits, transfer options), not commitments.
  • The clearest El Niño promise in this record is variance: the biggest winters (1983, 1998) were strong El Niños, but 2015–16 — the strongest event in the series — delivered a merely average year statewide. Pricing water transfers off the label alone repeats that mistake.
  • Wet-year capture is where the tilt pays: groundwater recharge (Flood-MAR-style diversions) turns the fat right tail into storage without betting on any single winter.

Reservoir operations: trust shorter forecasts

  • Flood-control releases hinge on individual atmospheric rivers, which ENSO does not schedule. Forecast-informed reservoir operations (FIRO, piloted at Lake Mendocino and Prado Dam) act on skillful 1–5 day forecasts instead of the seasonal label.
  • An El Niño winter is a reason to rehearse pre-releases and check encroachment rules early — not to hold the flood pool differently all season on the strength of a 0.19 R².
  • La Niña's dry tilt compounds across consecutive years (2021–22 in this record); multi-year drought sequencing, not any single winter, is what stresses carryover storage.

Nuclear siting: water as design basis

  • Reactors need a guaranteed heat sink, so licensing turns on extremes: design-basis flood on the wet side, low-water and cooling-supply reliability on the dry side. Those bounds must come from the full record across all ENSO phases plus trend — a phase-conditioned mean is exactly the wrong statistic.
  • Strong El Niño winters raise coastal sea level and wave energy along California (1982–83 and 1997–98 were landmark erosion winters), which is the relevant ENSO channel for a coastal, ocean-cooled site like Diablo Canyon — shoreline attack, not supply.
  • For inland, river- or reservoir-cooled sites, the La Niña side binds: sustained drought lowers intake elevations and tightens thermal-discharge limits, the mechanism behind heat-and-drought power derates seen at river-cooled plants in the U.S. and Europe.

New builds: decouple from the winter

  • Siting choices can buy independence from hydrology altogether: Palo Verde runs in the Sonoran Desert on treated municipal wastewater, and dry or hybrid cooling trades a few points of efficiency for indifference to what ENSO does.
  • For small modular reactor siting in the West, that trade is the honest hedge — a design that doesn't need the wet years never has to guess which label the winter will wear.
  • Same conclusion as the regressions, one level up: use the ENSO signal to time preparation, and engineer the things that can't fail so the signal doesn't matter.

These are planning considerations, not findings of this analysis — the models above are descriptive, statewide, and say nothing about any specific basin, shoreline, or site.
